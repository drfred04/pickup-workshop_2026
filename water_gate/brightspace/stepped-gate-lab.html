The Stepped Gate: Holding It Shut at the Hinge

A gate bent through two right angles holds back water. Its upper leg (height a) hangs from the bottom of a wall, bends away from the water into a horizontal center section (length b), then bends down into a lower leg (height c). The gate is L long into the page. It is carried at A, the midpoint of the center section, by a hinge with a locking actuator that can push in any direction and supply a holding moment. Water presses on three faces at once. You will draw the free-body diagram, replace each distributed water load with a resultant, write the equilibrium equations, and solve for the forces and moment the hinge must supply.

The water surface lies partway down the upper leg, a depth h above the center section. The wall above the gate and the top of the upper leg are dry. The gate touches nothing but the hinge: there is a small sealed gap at the wall (B) and at the floor (C), so neither can push on the gate. Ignore atmospheric pressure — it acts on both faces of the gate and cancels.

1. Build the free-body diagram

Isolate the gate. The loads are known, so give their true form and direction: the shape and direction of the water pressure on each wetted face, and the gate’s weight. The reactions at A — Ax, Ay and the holding moment MA — are unknowns, so you may assume either sense for each one. Your equations will tell you whether each assumption was right.

2. Replace each water load with its resultant

Each resultant equals the “volume” of its pressure distribution (area of the pressure diagram × length L) and acts through the centroid of that diagram. Call them F_1 (upper leg), F_2 (underside of the center section) and F_3 (lower leg).

Locate each line of action relative to the hinge A, as in the sketch: y_1 is measured up from the level of A to F_1, y_3 is measured down from the level of A to F_3, and x_2 is the horizontal distance from A to the line of action of F_2.

Write each in terms of rho, g, L, h, b, c. Use * for multiplication and ^ for powers, e.g. rho*g*L*h^2.

Upper leg: the pressure grows from zero at the water line to ρgh at the level of A, so the diagram is a triangle; its centroid is one-third of the way up from its wide end.
